# Time Detectives: Teacher Walkthrough

This answer key lists every decision in the game, the four possible answers with their tier and points, the feedback pupils see, and the in-world consequence shown for a misstep. Keep it away from pupils.

Tiers: Ideal = 100 pts; Plausible but incomplete = 65 pts; Passive / avoidant = 30 pts; Active misstep = 0 pts.
Points come from the final decision of each case. Earlier leads give formative feedback only. Cases you have not assigned are skipped automatically.

## Case 1: The Silent Ziggurat

**Ancient Mesopotamia** · Skill: **Sourcing** · Single decision

Agency dispatch: Ur's ziggurat records have been tampered with. One inscription says the king built it alone, and the tablet that says otherwise is missing. Check the source before you file anything.

**Scenario.** A scribe's tablet naming the true builder of Ur's ziggurat has gone missing. A royal inscription claims the king built it single-handed in one season.

**Civilisation briefing.** Mesopotamia — the land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers — is often called the cradle of cities. Its people built some of the world's first urban centres, developed cuneiform (the earliest known writing system), and created written law codes like Hammurabi's Code, which set out rules and punishments for everyone to see rather than leaving justice to a ruler's word alone. They also divided time and angles using a base-60 counting system, and organised large-scale irrigation to farm dry land.

### Decision

#### Decision (scored)

Prompt: You find a royal inscription boasting the king personally built the ziggurat in one season. What do you do?

- **Ideal (100 pts):** Royal inscriptions exist partly to glorify the king, so I'll check ration tablets before trusting the claim.
  - Feedback: Exactly right. This is sourcing: asking who made a record and why before trusting its content. Ration tablets show thousands of workers were fed for years — the inscription was royal propaganda, not a build log.
- **Plausible but incomplete (65 pts):** Kings typically funded these projects, so this account is probably close to true, even if some details sound exaggerated.
  - Feedback: You're on the right track noticing exaggeration, but you didn't check who wrote this or why — you assumed truth first and doubted second. Sourcing means checking the origin before deciding what to believe.
- **Passive / avoidant (30 pts):** It's carved by official palace scribes onto solid stone, so there's honestly not much more to think about here.
  - Feedback: No reasoning was applied here — the inscription's authority (being carved in stone) was taken as proof by itself. Nothing about the source's purpose was considered.
- **Active misstep (0 pts):** This is a genuine royal inscription in the king's own words, which makes it about as trustworthy an account as anyone could hope to find.
  - Feedback: This is naive realism: treating a source as true simply because it looks official. Royal inscriptions are exactly the kind of record most likely to exaggerate, since they exist to glorify the person who ordered them made.
  - What happens next: Your report is filed with the king's boast quoted as fact. When an archivist later matches it against the ration tablets, thousands of fed workers are missing from your version, and the file comes back stamped 'uncorroborated'.

### Knowledge check answers

- Reasoning: Why might a royal inscription be a less reliable record of who built something than a labourer's ration tablet? **Answer:** Inscriptions exist partly to glorify the king, while ration tablets record routine facts
- Reasoning: What question does 'sourcing' ask you to consider first? **Answer:** Who made this record, and why?
- History: What was Hammurabi's Code? **Answer:** An early written law code
- History: What counting system did Mesopotamia contribute that we still use today? **Answer:** A base-60 system (60 minutes, 360 degrees)

## Case 2: The Weighing of the Heart

**Ancient Egypt** · Skill: **Contextualization** · Full case (3 leads, comparison, final judgement)

Agency dispatch: A tomb painting was swapped for a replica overnight. The Agency needs to know what the original scene meant to the people who made it, not what we make of it now.

**Scenario.** A tomb painting showing a pharaoh's heart weighed against a feather has been replaced with a replica. To recover the original, you must show you understand what this ritual actually meant to the people who made it — not just judge it by today's standards.

**Civilisation briefing.** Ancient Egypt developed one of the world's longest-lasting civilisations along the Nile River, uniting Upper and Lower Egypt under pharaohs believed to bridge the human and divine worlds. Egyptians tracked the Nile's flooding with a 365-day solar calendar, developed hieroglyphic writing, and built monumental structures like the pyramids using advanced engineering and organised labour. Their religious beliefs about the afterlife, including judgement scenes like the weighing of the heart, shaped enormous investment in temples, tombs, and ritual across the civilisation's history.

### Lead: The Book of the Dead papyrus

A funerary papyrus shows the heart-weighing scene in detail: a person's heart on one side of a scale, the feather of Ma'at on the other.

#### Lead decision (formative)

Prompt: A modern reader calls this 'primitive superstition, not worth serious study.' How do you respond?

- **Ideal (100 pts):** Within Egyptian belief this represents a real moral idea about living truthfully in that era.
  - Feedback: Correct — this is contextualization. The scene reflects a moral framework about living truthfully, expressed in the religious language of its time, not a literal scientific claim.
- **Plausible but incomplete (65 pts):** It's clearly symbolic rather than literal, so calling it plain superstition seems a bit unfair to the culture that actually made it.
  - Feedback: You rightly avoided taking it literally, but didn't explain what it actually meant within Egyptian belief — you defended the source without fully contextualizing it.
- **Passive / avoidant (30 pts):** Old religious beliefs from this far back are hard to relate to, so it's not worth a strong opinion.
  - Feedback: No attempt was made to place this within Egyptian belief at all. Contextualization means engaging with the worldview, not stepping back from it.
- **Active misstep (0 pts):** By modern scientific standards this idea doesn't hold up, so calling it primitive seems like a fair call.
  - Feedback: This is presentism — judging the past using today's knowledge as if it applied then. Every belief system should be understood on its own terms first.
  - What happens next: Your note calls the scene primitive, so the Agency's reviewer strikes the papyrus from the case as unserious. The Egyptian idea behind it, that a life is judged on how truthfully it was lived, drops out of the record.

### Lead: A Greek visitor's account (Herodotus)

Herodotus, a Greek traveller, describes Egyptian embalming and afterlife rites in tones of amazement, written for readers back home who had no similar customs.

#### Lead decision (formative)

Prompt: Herodotus reacts to the rituals as strange and remarkable. What does that reaction actually tell you?

- **Ideal (100 pts):** His astonishment reflects Greek unfamiliarity with the practice, not proof that Egyptians themselves saw it as strange.
  - Feedback: Right — this separates an outsider's culturally-conditioned reaction from the practice's actual meaning inside Egyptian belief, where it was serious and central, not strange.
- **Plausible but incomplete (65 pts):** He's writing as an outsider looking in, so his account probably isn't the most accurate description available.
  - Feedback: Noticing he's an outsider is a start, but you didn't connect that to why his tone of amazement doesn't reflect how Egyptians themselves understood the ritual.
- **Passive / avoidant (30 pts):** Old traveller's writing from thousands of years ago is hard to make much sense of either way, honestly.
  - Feedback: No attempt was made to read the account in context. An outsider's perspective is still usable evidence once you account for its bias.
- **Active misstep (0 pts):** Since even a foreign visitor found it shocking, the ritual must have seemed strange by ancient standards too.
  - Feedback: This treats an outsider's culturally-specific reaction as if it were a universal or Egyptian judgement — a contextualization error. A Greek finding it foreign doesn't mean Egyptians did.
  - What happens next: You file the ritual as strange even in its own time, because a Greek visitor found it strange. Temple records show it was routine to the people who lived it, so your line is struck and your report is flagged.

### Lead: Temple administrative records

Ration and resource records show the state devoted enormous funding to funerary temples and priesthoods.

#### Lead decision (formative)

Prompt: A student says devoting this much of the state's resources to funerary temples was a 'strange priority.' How do you read this evidence?

- **Ideal (100 pts):** Egyptians believed maintaining the afterlife was essential state business, so this spending reflects real conviction.
  - Feedback: This correctly reads major resource allocation as a sign of what the state itself considered essential — understood in Egyptian terms, not modern budgeting priorities.
- **Plausible but incomplete (65 pts):** They clearly valued this a great deal, judging by how many resources were poured into it over time.
  - Feedback: Correctly notices the scale, but doesn't explain it through Egyptian belief about the afterlife's role in maintaining cosmic order.
- **Passive / avoidant (30 pts):** Administrative records this old are usually pretty dry reading and don't offer much of interest.
  - Feedback: The records were not engaged with as evidence at all, despite showing clearly what mattered to the state.
- **Active misstep (0 pts):** Spending this much on the afterlife while ordinary farmers still needed grain clearly shows the government had oddly mixed-up priorities that modern economists would rightly question.
  - Feedback: This is presentism — judging ancient resource allocation using modern economic priorities, rather than understanding it through the belief system that made it a state priority.
  - What happens next: You file that the priorities were 'mixed up'. The reviewer returns it: you judged a temple economy by modern budgets, so your report explains nothing about why the afterlife justified the expense.

### Comparing the accounts

#### Comparison decision (formative)

Prompt: You've now seen the ritual scene, an outsider's reaction to it, and the resources the state devoted to it. What do these three sources reveal together?

- **Ideal (100 pts):** Together they show this belief structured religion, spending, and daily life — best understood through Egyptian eyes, not outside judgement.
  - Feedback: This synthesises all three sources correctly — each on its own shows one angle, but together they demonstrate how central this belief truly was within its own society.
- **Plausible but incomplete (65 pts):** Taken as a whole, it does seem like the afterlife mattered quite a lot to Egyptian society generally.
  - Feedback: A reasonable overall impression, but it doesn't draw specifically on what each of the three sources actually contributed to that picture.
- **Passive / avoidant (30 pts):** Three separate old records like this are pretty hard to connect into any kind of clear overall picture.
  - Feedback: No attempt was made to compare what the sources show together, even though each supports the same underlying picture.
- **Active misstep (0 pts):** Since Herodotus found it strange, this was probably a fairly minor practice, whatever the temple records might suggest.
  - Feedback: This lets one outsider's reaction override strong evidence (state-level resourcing) that the practice was in fact deeply central — the wrong source was trusted over the stronger evidence.
  - What happens next: Your summary calls the weighing of the heart a minor practice. The next archivist opens the temple records, finds it treated as central, and reopens the case with your summary attached as a warning.

### Final judgement

#### Final decision (scored)

Prompt: The museum wants your final judgement: was the heart-weighing scene 'primitive superstition,' or something else?

- **Ideal (100 pts):** It expressed truth and balance, and 'primitive' misjudges a coherent belief system on its own terms.
  - Feedback: You've solved it. Understanding the ritual within Egyptian belief — not Greek astonishment or modern judgement — shows a coherent worldview about truth and balance that shaped Egyptian religion, architecture, and the calendar we still use variations of today.
- **Plausible but incomplete (65 pts):** It was clearly meaningful to the people who created and practised it, even if the underlying belief itself is hard to fully explain in modern terms today.
  - Feedback: You avoided calling it 'primitive,' which is good, but stopped short of explaining what the belief actually meant using the evidence you gathered.
- **Passive / avoidant (30 pts):** Ancient beliefs like this one are just ancient beliefs, and there isn't a lot more worth saying.
  - Feedback: This avoids engaging with the specific evidence gathered across the three leads, missing the chance to explain what was actually learned.
- **Active misstep (0 pts):** Modern science shows hearts can't be 'weighed' for truth, so calling this superstition seems fair.
  - Feedback: This is presentism, repeated at the final judgement — applying modern scientific standards to a belief system that operated on entirely different terms, ignoring everything the evidence showed about its real cultural role.
  - What happens next: Your verdict goes on file: superstition. Future detectives who open this case read a judgement in place of an explanation, and the Egyptian idea of truthful living never reaches the archive.

### Knowledge check answers

- Reasoning: What does 'contextualization' ask a historian to do with a ritual like heart-weighing? **Answer:** Understand it using the beliefs of its own time
- Reasoning: Why shouldn't Herodotus's astonishment be read as proof the ritual was seen as strange in Egypt itself? **Answer:** His reaction reflects Greek unfamiliarity, not Egyptian views
- Reasoning: What does heavy state spending on funerary temples suggest, read in Egyptian context? **Answer:** The afterlife was considered genuinely essential state business
- History: What natural event did Egypt's 365-day calendar help track? **Answer:** The annual flooding of the Nile
- History: What are obelisks, copied from Egyptian design, an example of today? **Answer:** Egyptian monuments echoed in cities like Paris and London today

## Case 3: The Rival Accounts

**Ancient Greece** · Skill: **Corroboration** · Full case (3 leads, comparison, final judgement)

Agency dispatch: Two Greek accounts tell different stories about the same events, and the neutral record that could settle it has been touched. Compare before you conclude.

**Scenario.** A trophy said to commemorate a decisive naval battle has vanished. Athens and Sparta each claim their fleet won — and neither account admits to losing anything.

**Civilisation briefing.** Ancient Greece was made up of independent city-states such as Athens and Sparta, each with its own government, army, and customs, but sharing a language, religion, and culture. Athens developed an early form of democracy where citizens voted directly on laws, while Greek thinkers laid foundations for Western philosophy, mathematics, and science. Greek city-states also held athletic festivals, including the Olympic Games at Olympia, and developed drama — tragedy and comedy performed in open-air theatres — as a major art form.

### Lead: Herodotus's account (Athenian-leaning)

Herodotus, writing from an Athenian perspective, describes the battle as a clear Athenian triumph.

#### Lead decision (formative)

Prompt: You've read the Athenian-leaning account. What do you do with it?

- **Ideal (100 pts):** This is one version, favouring Athens — I'll check for an independent account first.
  - Feedback: Correct — a single account, even a famous one, is a claim to check, not a fact to bank. This is corroboration in practice.
- **Plausible but incomplete (65 pts):** Herodotus is a widely respected historian, so this account is probably fairly close to what happened.
  - Feedback: Reputation isn't the same as independent confirmation — you assumed accuracy rather than checking it against another source first.
- **Passive / avoidant (30 pts):** That's one account read through, and honestly there doesn't seem to be much more worth considering there, so it's probably time to just move on to the next lead.
  - Feedback: No evaluation was made of the source at all before moving on.
- **Active misstep (0 pts):** Herodotus is often called 'the father of history,' so this account is essentially reliable as written.
  - Feedback: This is single-source over-reliance — a famous author's account still needs independent confirmation, especially when it favours his own city.
  - What happens next: You file Herodotus's Athenian-leaning account as reliable as written. The Agency's summary now repeats one side's version as settled, and the other city's story is never weighed against it.

### Lead: A Spartan-aligned historian's account

A rival historian, writing from a perspective aligned with Sparta, describes the same battle as a Spartan victory.

#### Lead decision (formative)

Prompt: This account flatly contradicts the first. What do you do?

- **Ideal (100 pts):** Both accounts favour their own side, so I need a source that has no stake in either city's version.
  - Feedback: Correctly recognises that two biased, opposing accounts can't resolve each other — a genuinely independent third source is needed.
- **Plausible but incomplete (65 pts):** This account includes more battle detail, so it might actually turn out to be the more accurate one.
  - Feedback: Detail doesn't establish accuracy, especially from a source with its own reason to favour Sparta.
- **Passive / avoidant (30 pts):** They disagree with each other completely, so it seems like we'll probably never really know for sure.
  - Feedback: Disagreement between biased sources is a reason to seek independent evidence, not a dead end.
- **Active misstep (0 pts):** Since this account is more recent, it's likely meant as a correction of errors Herodotus originally made.
  - Feedback: Being written later doesn't remove bias — this is still a single, self-interested source being trusted without independent confirmation.
  - What happens next: You file the later account as a 'correction' of Herodotus. Being later doesn't make it independent, so your note wrongly tells the next reader that the disagreement has already been resolved.

### Lead: A neutral city's dedication inscription

An inscription from a city not allied with either Athens or Sparta records tribute and casualty offerings connected to the battle.

#### Lead decision (formative)

Prompt: This source has no obvious reason to favour either side. How do you use it?

- **Ideal (100 pts):** I'll check exactly what this inscription records — casualties, tribute, dates — against what each side actually claims.
  - Feedback: This is the right move — treating even a neutral source's specific content as evidence to check against the biased accounts, not accepting it as automatically decisive.
- **Plausible but incomplete (65 pts):** Since this source is neutral, whatever it seems to suggest is probably close to the historical truth.
  - Feedback: Neutrality helps, but you still need to check its specific details rather than assuming its very existence settles the question.
- **Passive / avoidant (30 pts):** An inscription like this one is pretty hard to make sense of without more context to work from.
  - Feedback: The inscription's actual recorded content wasn't examined, despite being available, checkable evidence.
- **Active misstep (0 pts):** Since this source is neutral, it should simply count as a third vote alongside the other two accounts.
  - Feedback: Treating it as a 'vote' rather than checking its specific content skips the actual corroboration work this source could do.
  - What happens next: You count the neutral inscription as one more vote. Its value was that it stood outside the quarrel, and by tallying votes your report never says which claims it actually supports.

### Comparing the accounts

#### Comparison decision (formative)

Prompt: You now have three accounts: Athenian-leaning, Spartan-leaning, and a neutral inscription. How do you resolve the contradiction?

- **Ideal (100 pts):** I'll match the neutral inscription's details against each side's claims, since it alone favours neither.
  - Feedback: This is full corroboration: using the one genuinely independent source's specific details to adjudicate between two self-interested accounts, rather than picking a favourite.
- **Plausible but incomplete (65 pts):** The Athenian and neutral accounts seem to line up a bit more closely with one another overall, so Athens probably came out on top in this particular battle.
  - Feedback: You're using the right kind of evidence, but 'seem to line up' isn't the same as actually checking specific matching details.
- **Passive / avoidant (30 pts):** Three quite different accounts of the same battle is a hard thing to sort through clearly.
  - Feedback: This bypasses the entire comparison, which is the actual skill this case is testing.
- **Active misstep (0 pts):** The more dramatic and detailed of the three accounts is probably closer to what really happened.
  - Feedback: Drama and detail don't indicate accuracy — this ignores the neutral source entirely in favour of a vividness bias.
  - What happens next: You pick the most dramatic account as closest to the truth. The Agency's cross-check finds the vivid version carries the most unsupported detail, and your summary is marked 'persuasive, not proven'.

### Final judgement

#### Final decision (scored)

Prompt: The gallery wants your verdict: which city's fleet actually won, and what should the trophy plaque say?

- **Ideal (100 pts):** I'll report only what the neutral inscription's details support, noting the biased accounts alone aren't decisive.
  - Feedback: You've solved it. Corroborating a neutral, independent source against two competing biased accounts is how historians resolve exactly this kind of dispute — a method still used to check conflicting historical claims today.
- **Plausible but incomplete (65 pts):** Athens probably won this battle, since two of the three sources seem to lean that way overall.
  - Feedback: You used real evidence, but 'point that way overall' understates the need to check specific corroborating details rather than a general lean.
- **Passive / avoidant (30 pts):** It's genuinely unclear who actually won this one, so perhaps the plaque should simply note that historians still disagree on the matter.
  - Feedback: Historians disagreeing doesn't mean nothing can be concluded — the neutral inscription's specific details offer a real way to make progress.
- **Active misstep (0 pts):** Herodotus is the most famous source available, so his account should be treated as the final word.
  - Feedback: Fame is not evidence — this repeats single-source over-reliance at the final decision, ignoring the corroborating work the neutral inscription could do.
  - What happens next: You declare Herodotus the final word. An archivist holding the other two accounts challenges the stamped verdict within days, and the case reopens with your name on the reversed ruling.

### Knowledge check answers

- Reasoning: Why can't two opposing, self-interested accounts resolve a historical dispute on their own? **Answer:** Both have reason to favour their own side
- Reasoning: What makes the third inscription especially useful for corroboration? **Answer:** It has no obvious reason to favour either side
- Reasoning: What's the strongest way to use a neutral source to resolve a conflicting claim? **Answer:** Check its specific details against each side's claims
- History: What made Athens' system of government especially notable in the ancient world? **Answer:** Citizens could vote directly on laws
- History: Where were the ancient Olympic Games held? **Answer:** Olympia

## Case 4: The Fall of the Aqueduct

**Ancient Rome** · Skill: **Causation vs. correlation** · Single decision

Agency dispatch: An aqueduct has collapsed and everyone has a theory. The Anachronist would like the wrong one to reach the archive.

**Scenario.** A Roman aqueduct collapsed the same week a new emperor took power. A pamphlet claims the new emperor's 'bad luck' caused the collapse.

**Civilisation briefing.** At its height, the Roman Empire stretched across Europe, North Africa, and the Middle East, held together by a shared system of law, roads, and administration. Roman law — the idea of codified, applied legal principles — influenced legal systems across the world. Roman engineers built an extensive network of roads, aqueducts (structures carrying water over long distances), and used a durable form of concrete, allowing large cities to thrive far from natural water sources. Latin, the language of Rome, is the ancestor of French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, and Romanian.

### Decision

#### Decision (scored)

Prompt: The pamphlet blames the new emperor's arrival for the collapse. How do you investigate the real cause?

- **Ideal (100 pts):** I'll check maintenance records for an actual mechanism, like unrepaired cracks, rather than trusting the timing alone.
  - Feedback: This correctly separates timing from causation. Maintenance records show the aqueduct had unrepaired structural cracks for years — a real, documented mechanism, unrelated to the emperor's arrival.
- **Plausible but incomplete (65 pts):** The timing is probably coincidence, though maybe political chaos led to maintenance being neglected, which caused it.
  - Feedback: You correctly doubted the superstition, and this contributing factor is plausible — but you stated it as the cause without checking maintenance records to confirm it.
- **Passive / avoidant (30 pts):** Big old structures like aqueducts do collapse from time to time, so there's no real need to dig deeper.
  - Feedback: No cause was investigated at all. Explaining collapses requires looking at evidence, not shrugging at coincidence.
- **Active misstep (0 pts):** The aqueduct collapsed in the very same week that the new emperor arrived in the city, which surely can't just be an accident of timing and points to his rule as the true cause.
  - Feedback: This is correlation mistaken for causation — assuming that because B followed A, A caused B. A real mechanism (like structural failure) needs to be shown, not just a shared timeline.
  - What happens next: Your report blames the new emperor because the collapse fell in the week he arrived. A later inspection points to a slower cause that the timing hid, and the real problem stays unaddressed in your file.

### Knowledge check answers

- Reasoning: What's the key difference between causation and correlation? **Answer:** Causation requires a documented mechanism linking cause to effect; correlation is just events occurring together
- Reasoning: Two events happening in the same week is enough to prove one caused the other. **Answer:** False — a mechanism must be shown
- History: What is a defining feature of Roman aqueducts? **Answer:** They carried water across long distances
- History: Which modern languages descend from Latin, the language of Rome? **Answer:** French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, and Romanian

## Case 5: The Grid City

**Indus Valley Civilisation** · Skill: **Close reading of primary evidence** · Single decision

Agency dispatch: The Indus grid city is almost too orderly. The Agency wants a detective who reads exactly what the evidence shows, then stops.

**Scenario.** Excavators uncover the ruins of Mohenjo-daro's street grid and drainage system. A report claims this proves the city had an all-powerful king ruling by force.

**Civilisation briefing.** The Indus Valley Civilisation flourished along the Indus River around 2600-1900 BCE, with major cities like Mohenjo-daro and Harappa. Unlike many early civilisations, its cities followed remarkably organised grid layouts with covered drainage systems running beneath the streets — an early example of urban planning. Traders used standardised weights and carved stone seals, suggesting widespread, organised commerce. Despite extensive archaeology, the Indus script has never been deciphered, so much about its government and beliefs remains genuinely unknown.

### Decision

#### Decision (scored)

Prompt: A report claims the organised grid proves an all-powerful king ruled by force. What does the physical evidence actually support?

- **Ideal (100 pts):** The grid and drainage show organised planning and shared standards — not who held power, since no palace has been found.
  - Feedback: Correct close reading — the evidence supports organisation and planning, but says nothing about how power was held, since none of the usual signs of a powerful ruler (palaces, grand tombs) have been found here.
- **Plausible but incomplete (65 pts):** Such an organised city probably had some form of central authority, even if we can't be sure it was a king.
  - Feedback: A reasonable inference, but you're adding a conclusion (central authority) that the physical evidence alone doesn't confirm — the grid shows planning, not who planned it or how.
- **Passive / avoidant (30 pts):** It's a pretty old city, so it's hard to say much of anything specific about how it actually worked.
  - Feedback: The evidence itself was barely examined. A street grid and drainage system are detailed physical evidence that can support real conclusions about planning.
- **Active misstep (0 pts):** Only a powerful king with total control over the population could possibly have organised something this precise and orderly across an entire city.
  - Feedback: This is over-reading: projecting a conclusion the evidence doesn't contain. Organised planning can come from shared civic standards or councils, not only a single all-powerful ruler — and no royal monuments back up the king theory.
  - What happens next: You file that only an all-powerful king could have planned the grid. Nothing found in the city points to a king's palace or throne, so your report is sent back for claiming more than the evidence shows.

### Knowledge check answers

- Reasoning: What does the Indus Valley's grid layout and drainage directly provide evidence of? **Answer:** Organised urban planning
- Reasoning: Why is it risky to assume a powerful king built Mohenjo-daro's grid? **Answer:** No palace or royal monuments have been found there
- History: What is Mohenjo-daro's street layout notable for? **Answer:** An organised grid with covered drainage
- History: What is still true about the Indus script today? **Answer:** It has never been deciphered

## Case 6: The Fire Altar

**Vedic / Indo-Aryan Period** · Skill: **Contextualization** · Single decision

Agency dispatch: A fire altar is being described as crude and improvised. Read it against its own time before that description hardens into fact.

**Scenario.** A hymn from the Rig Veda describes elaborate fire rituals with precise chants. A modern reader calls it 'just superstition around a campfire.'

**Civilisation briefing.** Following the decline of the Indus Valley cities, Indo-Aryan-speaking peoples became dominant across northern India from around 1500 BCE, a period known for the composition of the Vedas — the oldest Hindu scriptures, preserved through exact oral memorisation for centuries before ever being written down. This era established Sanskrit, the classical language of Hindu ritual and Indian scholarship, and developed early philosophical and religious ideas — including fire rituals believed to maintain cosmic order — that shaped Hindu practice for millennia afterward.

### Decision

#### Decision (scored)

Prompt: A modern reader dismisses the fire ritual hymns as unsophisticated. How should this evidence be understood?

- **Ideal (100 pts):** Vedic society believed that precise ritual performance, especially exact chanting, was essential to maintaining cosmic order itself.
  - Feedback: This places the practice in its own worldview: a society valuing precise oral transmission and ritual accuracy for cosmic reasons — not mere 'campfire superstition.'
- **Plausible but incomplete (65 pts):** It's clearly more sophisticated than 'superstition,' since so much evident care went into how it was performed.
  - Feedback: You're right to push back, but without explaining the Vedic worldview itself (cosmic order through precise ritual), the defence is incomplete.
- **Passive / avoidant (30 pts):** Ancient religion from this far back is hard to relate to, so it's best not to comment much further.
  - Feedback: No engagement with the belief system occurred. Contextualization means stepping into that worldview, not avoiding it.
- **Active misstep (0 pts):** Without written records to keep it precise, the rituals were probably just improvised loosely each time.
  - Feedback: This is presentism — assuming that without modern written records, transmission must have been sloppy. Oral tradition in Vedic society was in fact a highly disciplined, precise system of memorisation.
  - What happens next: You file the rituals as loosely improvised. The precision of the altar's layout and the carefully memorised chants contradict you, and your line is struck as an assumption the evidence never supported.

### Knowledge check answers

- Reasoning: Why was precise chanting so important in Vedic ritual, according to their own worldview? **Answer:** It was believed essential to cosmic order
- Reasoning: What is 'oral tradition'? **Answer:** Knowledge passed down through memorised speech
- History: What are the Vedas? **Answer:** The oldest Hindu scriptures
- History: How were the Vedas preserved before being written down? **Answer:** Precise oral memorisation passed down through generations

## Case 7: The Golden Age Claim
